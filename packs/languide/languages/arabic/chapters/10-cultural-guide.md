# Cultural Guide

This chapter explains the cultural facts that change what you say: which variety of Arabic to use, which fixed reply to give, how to accept and refuse, and how to address people. It does not give sightseeing advice.

---

## One Language, Two Layers

Every Arabic speaker lives with two forms of the language. This situation is called **diglossia**.

| Layer | Arabic name | Learned | Used for |
|-------|-------------|---------|----------|
| Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) | الفصحى (al-fuṣḥā) | At school | Writing, news, speeches, formal meetings, religion, education |
| Dialect | العامية (al-ʿāmmiyya) | At home, as the mother tongue | All normal conversation |

### What This Means for a Learner

- **MSA is the same everywhere.** A report from Morocco and a contract from Kuwait use the same language.
- **Nobody speaks pure MSA at home.** A person who speaks only MSA sounds formal, but people understand every word.
- **Dialects are different from country to country.** The difference is mainly in common small words: "what", "now", "I want", "not".
- **Educated speakers mix the two layers.** In a meeting, a speaker uses MSA vocabulary with dialect grammar. This mixed style is the real spoken language of work.
- **People adapt to you.** If you speak MSA, most people answer in a slow, clear mix that is close to MSA.

### Which Layer to Choose

| Situation | Best choice | Reason |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| Email, letters, reports | MSA | Dialect in a work document looks unprofessional |
| A presentation or a formal meeting | MSA with dialect greetings | It shows respect and education |
| Coffee with colleagues | Dialect | MSA sounds distant |
| Shops, taxis, restaurants | Dialect | It is faster and friendlier. MSA is understood |
| A country where you do not know the dialect | MSA | It is neutral and safe |
| Chat messages | Dialect, often in Arabizi | See Chapter 4 |

This guide gives Levantine as the dialect layer. If you work with people from Egypt or the Gulf, learn the 20 words in the comparison table below.

## The Dialect Groups

| Group | Countries | What a learner must know |
|-------|-----------|--------------------------|
| Levantine | Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Palestine | Close to MSA. Understood in most Arab countries because of television drama and music |
| Egyptian | Egypt | The most widely understood dialect, because of Egyptian film and television. The letter ج is a hard g. The letter ق is a hamza |
| Gulf | Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, Oman | Important for business. The letter ق is often a g. Many workplaces also use English |
| Iraqi | Iraq | Related to Gulf Arabic. It has its own common words, for example أكو (akū) "there is" |
| Maghrebi | Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya | Fast, with many short vowels dropped, and with French and Amazigh words. Speakers from the east find it difficult. Maghrebi speakers usually change to MSA or a mixed style with other Arabs |

Sudan and Yemen have their own dialects. They are nearer to the eastern group than to the Maghrebi group.

## Religious Formulas in Everyday Speech

Arabic conversation has many short formulas that name God. They are part of normal polite speech. Muslims and Christians use most of them, and they do not show how religious the speaker is. A learner can use them. People are pleased when a learner uses them correctly.

### The Three Most Common Formulas

| English | Arabic | Transliteration | Notes |
|---------|--------|-----------------|-------|
| God willing | إن شاء الله | in shāʾ allāh | Said with every statement about the future. It can mean "yes, I intend to", "I hope so", or a soft "maybe" |
| Praise be to God | الحمد لله | al-ḥamdu lillāh | The answer to "How are you?". Also said after a meal, after good news, and after a sneeze |
| What God has willed | ما شاء الله | mā shāʾ allāh | Said when you admire a child, a home, a new car, or a success. It shows that you praise without envy |

### What "In Shāʾ Allāh" Means in Practice

- When a colleague says "I will send the report tomorrow, in shāʾ allāh", it is a normal promise. The formula is a habit, not a sign of doubt.
- When it is the only answer to a request, it can be a polite way to avoid "no".
- If you need a firm commitment, ask a specific question: "At what time?" or "Which day?"
- Say it yourself when you talk about your plans. A future statement without it sounds too certain to many listeners.

### The Greeting Ritual

A greeting in Arabic is long. People do not go directly to the subject, also at work.

1. Give the greeting and wait for the reply.
2. Ask "How are you?". The other person asks you too.
3. Ask a second question: "How is the family?" or "How is work?"
4. Answer each question with الحمد لله (al-ḥamdu lillāh). A long, true answer is not expected.
5. Then start the subject.

Details that affect what you do:

- **Greet everybody.** When you enter a room, say السلام عليكم to the group. In a small group, greet each person.
- **Stand up** when you greet a person who enters.
- **Handshakes.** Men shake hands with men, and women with women. Between a man and a woman, wait: some people do not shake hands with the other sex. If the other person puts a hand on the chest, do the same and smile.
- **Ask about "the family"**, not about a man's wife specifically.

## Hospitality and Refusing Politely

Hospitality is a duty and a pleasure for the host. The guest also has a role.

- **An offer is repeated.** A host offers food or drink several times. A first "no, thank you" is a polite form. The host offers again.
- **Accept something small.** If you accept a coffee or a tea, the host is satisfied.
- **A real refusal needs a reason and thanks.** Say that you are full, or that you just ate, and praise God. A hand on the chest makes the refusal warm.
- **The host says "eat more".** Leave a little food on the plate to show that you are full.
- **A compliment on an object** can cause the owner to offer it to you. Refuse with thanks.
- **Paying the bill.** People compete to pay in a restaurant. Offer to pay, accept if the host insists, and say that the next time is yours.

| Tier | Address | Example | Transliteration |
|------|---------|---------|-----------------|
| Casual | First name, أنت | كيف حالك يا سمير؟ | kayfa ḥāluka yā samīr? |

## Communication Style

- **The relationship comes first.** People prefer to know a person before they do business. Small talk is not lost time.
- **A direct "no" is rare.** People prefer a soft answer that keeps the relationship comfortable.
- **Criticism is private.** Do not correct a person in front of the group.
- **Praise is generous.** Compliments on food, work and hospitality are normal and expected.
- **Safe subjects** are food, family in general, football, travel, and your Arabic studies.
- **Difficult subjects** are politics and religious debate. Listen, and do not start these subjects.

## Weekend and Working Hours

The working week is different from the week in Europe and North America. Friday is the day of the main weekly prayer in Islam.

### The Working Week by Country

| Countries | Working week | Weekend |
|-----------|--------------|---------|
| Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman, Egypt, Jordan, Iraq, Syria, Algeria, Libya | Sunday to Thursday | Friday and Saturday |
| The United Arab Emirates | Monday to Friday. Friday is a half day in government offices | Saturday and Sunday |
| Lebanon, Morocco, Tunisia | Monday to Friday | Saturday and Sunday |

Private companies can have different hours. Ask the company.

### What to Know About Time

- **Friday at noon** is the time of the Friday prayer. Do not plan a meeting or a call at that time in a country where Friday is a working day or a half day.
- **Prayer times** are five times a day. Some shops close for a short time, mainly in Saudi Arabia. A colleague can leave a meeting for a few minutes.
- **Ramadan** changes the day. Working hours are shorter. Most people do not eat or drink from dawn to sunset, and in many countries nobody eats or drinks in public in the daytime. Business meals are in the evening.
- **The two Eid feasts** are public holidays of several days. Offices close.
- **Two calendars** are in use. Business uses the Gregorian calendar. Religious holidays follow the Islamic lunar calendar, so their Gregorian dates change each year.
- **Punctuality.** Business meetings start near the stated time. Social invitations are more flexible.
