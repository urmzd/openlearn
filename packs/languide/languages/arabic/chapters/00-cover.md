# Arabic Communication Guide

### Practical Phrases for Everyday Conversation and Work in Modern Standard and Levantine Arabic

**Version 1.0, November 2026**

---

## About This Guide

This guide teaches **practical communication** in Arabic. It gives you the phrases and patterns that you need to talk to people, read short texts, and work with Arabic-speaking colleagues. The content is organized by situation. Every phrase is in a table, so you can scan, cover a column, and test yourself.

The guide is written for a learner, not for a visitor. It does not contain sightseeing advice. It contains language: what to say, how to say it, how to write it, and when it is appropriate.

## Who This Guide Is For

- An English-speaking adult who starts Arabic from zero
- A learner who studies about 30 minutes a day
- A learner who wants casual conversation with friends, neighbours and colleagues
- A learner who also needs Arabic at work: meetings, email, calls and office talk
- A learner who prefers scenarios and patterns to long grammar lessons

## Two Layers: Modern Standard Arabic and Levantine

Arabic is not one spoken language. It has two layers, and every Arabic speaker uses both. Linguists call this **diglossia**.

| Layer | Arabic name | Where you meet it | Role in this guide |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|--------------------|
| Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) | الفصحى (al-fuṣḥā) | Writing, news, email, contracts, signs, presentations, formal speech | The base form in every table |
| Spoken dialect | العامية (al-ʿāmmiyya) | Home, friends, shops, taxis, coffee breaks, chat messages | Levantine forms in the Notes column |

MSA is the same in every Arab country. Nobody speaks it at home, but everybody reads it and understands it. It is the language of business documents. If you learn MSA first, you can read, write, and be understood everywhere.

**Levantine Arabic** is the spoken dialect of Syria, Lebanon, Jordan and Palestine. It is close to MSA, it is widely understood, and it is a practical first dialect. This guide adds the Levantine form when everyday speech is different from MSA.

### How the Two Layers Appear in the Tables

| English | Arabic | Transliteration | Notes |
|---------|--------|-----------------|-------|
| I want coffee | أريد قهوة | urīd qahwa | Levantine: بدي قهوة (biddī ʾahwe) |
| Where is the station? | أين المحطة؟ | ayna al-maḥaṭṭa? | Levantine: وين المحطة؟ (wēn il-maḥaṭṭa?) |
| How much is this? | بكم هذا؟ | bi-kam hādhā? | Levantine: قديش هاد؟ (ʾaddēsh hād?) |
| I don't know | لا أعرف | lā aʿrif | Levantine: ما بعرف (mā baʿrif) |

- The **Arabic** and **Transliteration** columns are always MSA.
- A note that starts with **Levantine:** gives the everyday spoken form.
- If a row has no Levantine note, the MSA form also works in speech, or the difference is only in the vowels.

### Which Layer to Use

| Situation | Use |
|-----------|-----|
| Email, reports, slides, contracts | MSA |
| A formal meeting, a presentation, a speech | MSA, or MSA mixed with dialect |
| Reading signs, menus, forms, software | MSA |
| Small talk before a meeting | Dialect |
| Shops, taxis, restaurants | Dialect, but MSA is understood |
| Chat messages with colleagues | Dialect, often mixed with English |
| You do not know the local dialect | MSA. People answer in a simpler, clearer dialect |

A beginner who speaks MSA sounds formal, like a person who speaks from a book. That is acceptable. People understand you, and they respect the effort.

## How to Use This Guide

A study session of 30 minutes can follow this routine.

| Minutes | Activity | How |
|---------|----------|-----|
| 5 | Review | Do the Practice table of the last chapter that you studied. Cover the right column |
| 10 | New phrases | Read one table aloud, row by row. Read the Arabic script, not only the transliteration |
| 10 | Produce | Cover the Arabic and transliteration columns. Say each phrase from the English. Then swap words in two pattern templates |
| 5 | Script | Copy five words by hand, or type them on an Arabic keyboard |

## Key Features

### Pattern Phrases (Templates)

A **pattern phrase** is a template. You change the word in the `[brackets]` and you get a new sentence.

| Pattern | Arabic | Transliteration | Example |
|---------|--------|-----------------|---------|
| [thing] please | [شيء] من فضلك | [shayʾ] min faḍlika | قهوة من فضلك (qahwa min faḍlika) = Coffee please |
| Where is [place]? | أين [مكان]؟ | ayna [makān]? | أين المكتب؟ (ayna al-maktab?) = Where is the office? |
| I need [thing] | أحتاج إلى [شيء] | aḥtāj ilā [shayʾ] | أحتاج إلى مساعدة (aḥtāj ilā musāʿada) = I need help |

### Phrase Tables

All phrases use one **table format**.

| English | Arabic | Transliteration | Notes |
|---------|--------|-----------------|-------|
| Thank you | شكرا | shukran | All situations |
| Excuse me | لو سمحت | law samaḥta | Getting attention. To a woman: law samaḥti |

### Politeness Levels

| Tier | Use with | Typical markers |
|------|----------|-----------------|
| Casual | Friends, family, close colleagues of your age | أنت (anta) "you", direct requests, dialect |
| Polite | Strangers, staff, new colleagues. This is the default | من فضلك (min faḍlika), لو سمحت (law samaḥta), حضرتك (ḥaḍratuka) |
| Very polite | Senior people, clients, formal letters | لو تكرمت (law takarramta), titles, plural "you" |

### Gender in the Tables

Arabic changes some words when you speak to a woman, and some words when the speaker is a woman.

- The main columns give the form that you say **to a man**.
- A note **to a woman:** gives the form that you say to a woman.
- A note **woman speaking:** gives the form that a woman says about herself.

## How to Read the Transliteration

The transliteration shows careful pronunciation. Chapter 3 explains every sound. This table is a short key.

| Symbol | Arabic letter | Sound |
|--------|---------------|-------|
| ā, ī, ū | ا, ي, و | Long vowels. Hold them twice as long as a, i, u |
| ʾ | ء | A catch in the throat, as in the middle of "uh-oh" |
| ʿ | ع | A voiced squeeze deep in the throat |
| ḥ | ح | A strong, breathy h |
| kh | خ | As in Scottish "loch" |
| gh | غ | As the French r |
| q | ق | A k from the back of the mouth |
| ṣ, ḍ, ṭ, ẓ | ص, ض, ط, ظ | Heavy s, d, t, and dh |
| th, dh | ث, ذ | As in "think" and "this" |
| sh | ش | As in "ship" |
| ē, ō | none | Long e and o. Levantine forms and foreign words only |

Arabic script in this guide has no vowel marks, because real Arabic text has none. Chapters 3 and 4 are the exceptions. They show the vowel marks so that you can learn them.

## A Note on Accuracy and Variation

The Levantine forms are from the common urban speech of Damascus, Beirut, Amman and Jerusalem. Each city has small differences. Chapter 10 compares Levantine with Egyptian and Gulf Arabic for 20 key words.

If a person answers you with a word that is not in this guide, ask this question.

| English | Arabic | Transliteration | Notes |
|---------|--------|-----------------|-------|
| What does this word mean? | ما معنى هذه الكلمة؟ | mā maʿnā hādhihi al-kalima? | Levantine: شو يعني هاي الكلمة؟ (shū yaʿnī hāy il-kilme?) |
