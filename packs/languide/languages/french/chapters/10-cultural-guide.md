# Cultural Guide

This chapter covers the customs that change what you say: the required greeting, tu and vous, the kiss and the handshake, and the vocabulary that separates France from Quebec. It ends with the language laws that shape signs and working life.

---

## Bonjour: The Required Opener

In France and in Quebec, every interaction starts with a greeting. A question with no "Bonjour" sounds like an order. Service can turn cold if you skip it.

| Situation | What to say first | Notes |
|-----------|-------------------|-------|
| You enter a small shop, a bakery or a pharmacy | Bonjour | Say it to the room, even if staff are busy |
| You ask a stranger for directions | Bonjour, excusez-moi | Then ask the question |
| You step up to a counter or a desk | Bonjour | Before you state what you want |
| You get into a taxi or onto a bus | Bonjour | To the driver |
| You enter a lift or a waiting room with people in it | Bonjour | France: usual. Quebec: less usual |
| You join a call or a meeting | Bonjour à tous | See Chapter 11 |
| You write an email or a chat message | Bonjour | Never start with the request |
| It is after about 6 PM | Bonsoir | Replaces "Bonjour" |
| You leave | Au revoir, bonne journée | The closing is as expected as the opening |

| English | French | Pronunciation | Notes |
|---------|--------|---------------|-------|
| Hello, I would like a baguette | Bonjour, je voudrais une baguette | bohn-zhoor zhuh voo-dreh ewn bah-get | Greeting first, request second |
| Hello sir, excuse me | Bonjour monsieur, excusez-moi | bohn-zhoor muh-syuh ex-kew-zay mwah | Polite. To a man |
| Hello madam | Bonjour madame | bohn-zhoor mah-dam | Polite. To any adult woman |
| Hello again | Rebonjour | ruh-bohn-zhoor | When you meet the same person a second time that day |
| Thank you, goodbye, have a good day | Merci, au revoir, bonne journée | mehr-see oh ruh-vwahr bon zhoor-nay | Standard closing |
| You too | Vous aussi | voo-zoh-see | Answer to "Bonne journée". Casual: "Toi aussi" |
| Hello, hi | Bonjour, hi | bohn-zhoor hye | Quebec: a bilingual greeting used in central Montreal shops. Answer in the language you want |

**Madame and Mademoiselle:** Use "Madame" for every adult woman. France removed "Mademoiselle" from official forms in 2012, and Quebec style guides advise against it.

## Tu and Vous

### France Versus Quebec

| Situation | France | Quebec |
|-----------|--------|--------|
| Strangers of your own age | vous | vous, with a quick move to tu |
| Shop and restaurant staff | vous in both directions | vous to staff. Staff may say tu to you in casual places |
| An older person | vous | vous |
| Friends of friends at a party | tu | tu |
| Colleagues | tu in most teams, sometimes after an invitation | tu from the first day |
| Your manager | vous until the manager proposes tu | tu in most workplaces |
| Clients and suppliers | vous | vous at first, tu once the relationship is friendly |
| Teachers and professors | vous | vous or tu, depending on the school |
| Government offices, police, doctors | vous | vous |
| Advertising and apps | vous, sometimes tu for young brands | tu is very common |

### Rules of Thumb

- **Start with vous.** Let the other person propose tu.
- **Mirror the other person.** If they say tu to you and they are about your age or level, say tu back.
- **In France, the older or more senior person proposes tu.** A refusal is rare but possible.
- **In Quebec, tu is not a sign of disrespect.** A server who says tu to you is being friendly.
- **Vous to one person, first name included, is normal.** "Vous" with a first name is common at work in France.
- **A slip is not serious.** Say "Pardon" and continue.

## Greetings with the Body: La Bise and the Handshake

"La bise" is a light kiss on each cheek, or cheek touching cheek with a kissing sound. It is a greeting between people who know each other. It is not romantic.

| Situation | France | Quebec |
|-----------|--------|--------|
| First meeting in a professional setting | Handshake | Handshake |
| Colleagues each morning | Handshake or a spoken "Bonjour". La bise in some teams | A spoken "Bonjour" or "Salut". No physical greeting |
| Friends and family | La bise, usually two kisses | Two kisses, or a hug |
| Friends of friends at a social event | La bise is common | A handshake, a wave or two kisses |
| Men greeting men | Handshake. La bise between close friends and family | Handshake or a hug between close friends |
| Clients | Handshake | Handshake |
| Leaving | The same gesture as on arrival | The same gesture as on arrival |

- **Number of kisses:** Two is the standard in most of France and in Quebec. Some French regions give one, three or four. Follow the other person.
- **Which side first:** It varies. Move slowly and follow.
- **If you prefer not to:** Offer your hand first. A handshake is always correct.
- **Hugs:** A hug is normal between friends in Quebec. In France, a hug is more intimate than la bise.

| English | French | Pronunciation | Notes |
|---------|--------|---------------|-------|
| Shall we do la bise? | On se fait la bise? | ohn suh feh lah beez | Casual. Removes the doubt |
| Nice to meet you | Enchanté / Enchantée | ahn-shahn-tay | With a handshake |
| I have a cold, I won't kiss you | Je suis enrhumé, je ne te fais pas la bise | zhuh swee ahn-rew-may zhuh nuh tuh feh pah lah beez | A polite way to skip it |
| Kisses | Bises / Bisous | beez / bee-zoo | Closing of a message to friends, never at work |

## Anglicisms

France and Quebec both borrow from English, but in opposite ways.

- **France** borrows English nouns freely in daily and business speech: le week-end, le parking, le mail, le meeting, le planning, le feedback.
- **Quebec** replaces those nouns with French terms in writing and in formal speech: la fin de semaine, le stationnement, le courriel. Casual Quebec speech then borrows other English words, often as verbs: checker, canceller, booker.

| English | France, everyday | Quebec, formal | Quebec, casual |
|---------|------------------|----------------|----------------|
| Weekend | le week-end | la fin de semaine | la fin de semaine |
| Email | le mail | le courriel | le courriel, le mail |
| Parking | le parking | le stationnement | le parking, le stationnement |
| Shopping | le shopping | le magasinage | le magasinage |
| To check | vérifier | vérifier | checker |
| To cancel | annuler | annuler | canceller |
| To book | réserver | réserver | booker |
| Deadline | la deadline | l'échéance, la date limite | le deadline |
| Feedback | le feedback, le retour | la rétroaction | le feedback |
| Fun | fun, sympa | amusant | le fun |
| Job | le job | l'emploi | la job |
| Cool | cool | super | cool, le fun |

In a formal Quebec setting, prefer the French term. In France, the French term is always correct, even when colleagues use the English one.

## Language Law: Signs and Work

This section is a plain summary for learners. It is not legal advice.

### Quebec: The Charter of the French Language

French is the only official language of Quebec. The Charter of the French Language, known as Bill 101 (1977), was strengthened in 2022 by a law known as Bill 96. The Office québécois de la langue française (OQLF) oversees it.

| Area | What the law requires | What you will notice |
|------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| Public signs and advertising | Signs must be in French. Another language may appear, but French must be markedly predominant. | French text is larger or more prominent. Road signs are in French only: "ARRÊT" |
| Brand names on shopfronts | A trademark in another language must appear with French text that is markedly predominant. | English brand names carry a French description, such as "café" or "vêtements" |
| Service to customers | Customers have the right to be informed and served in French. | Staff greet you in French first. In central Montreal you may hear "Bonjour, hi" |
| Products | Labels, instructions and warranties must be available in French. | Packaging is bilingual or French |
| Language of work | Employees have the right to work in French. | Meetings, tools and internal messages are in French by default |
| Work documents | Job offers, contracts, policies and training documents must be available in French. | You receive a French version of every official work document |
| Requiring another language | An employer may require a language other than French only when the job needs it and the employer can justify it. | Job adverts state why English is needed, when it is |
| Larger employers | Businesses above a set number of employees must register with the OQLF and show that French is the normal language of work. The threshold was lowered from 50 to 25 employees in 2025. | Software and keyboards in French, French job titles, French terminology lists |

What this means for a learner at work in Quebec:

- Expect French in writing, even in teams that speak English together.
- Use the official French terms: courriel, réunion, clavardage, télécharger, logiciel.
- The OQLF publishes a free online dictionary of work and technology terms, the "Grand dictionnaire terminologique".

### France: The Toubon Law

France has protected French in public and working life since the Toubon Law of 1994.

| Area | What the law requires | What you will notice |
|------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| Advertising | Text in another language must come with a French translation. | English slogans carry an asterisk and a small French translation |
| Consumer information | Labels, manuals and warranties must be in French. | Packaging is in French, often with other languages too |
| Employment | Employment contracts and documents an employee needs for the job must be in French. | Your contract is in French, even in an international firm |
| Official vocabulary | A state commission publishes French terms for new concepts. Public bodies must use them. | Official documents say "courriel". Colleagues say "mail" in speech |

In daily office speech, France is relaxed about English words. In contracts, payslips and official letters, everything is French.
