# Mandarin Chinese Communication Guide

### Practical Phrases for Everyday Conversation and Work

**Version 1.0, November 2026**

---

## About This Guide

This guide focuses on **practical communication**: the phrases and patterns a learner needs for real conversations in Mandarin Chinese. It teaches standard mainland Putonghua (普通话, Pǔtōnghuà), written in simplified characters, with pinyin and tone marks beside every phrase. Where usage in Taiwan changes the phrase, the guide gives both forms.

You will not find tourism tips here. You will find scenario-based phrases in tables, reusable pattern templates, and a practice table at the end of every chapter.

## Who This Guide Is For

- English-speaking adults who start from zero or near zero
- Learners who study in short daily sessions of about 30 minutes
- People who want casual conversation with friends, neighbors and colleagues
- People who need enough Mandarin for professional use: meetings, messages, calls and business meals
- Learners who prefer practical scenarios to grammar theory

## What's Inside

- **Pattern phrase templates:** Reusable structures such as "Where is [place]?" with slots you fill in
- **Scenario-based phrases:** Organized by situation (greetings, directions, transport, restaurants, shopping, hotels, work)
- **Communication tables:** English, simplified Chinese, pinyin with tone marks, and usage notes
- **Politeness tiers:** Casual (你), polite (您 and softeners such as 请 and 麻烦你), and very polite or formal
- **Pronunciation and tones:** The four tones, tone changes, and the sounds English speakers get wrong
- **Characters:** How characters work, 40 common radicals, 120 survival characters, and pinyin input
- **Work and business:** Titles, meetings, email, calls, WeChat, toasts and indirect refusals
- **Practice tables:** Ten recall prompts at the end of each chapter

## How to Use This Guide

1. **Start with Chapter 3 (Pronunciation).** Tones change meaning. Learn them before you learn many words.
2. **Learn Chapter 1 (Core Phrases) next.** The pattern templates give you the most sentences for the least effort.
3. **Read Chapter 2 (Language Foundations) in small parts.** Use it to understand why the patterns work.
4. **Move to the scenario chapters you need first.** Each one stands alone.
5. **Read each phrase aloud.** Read the pinyin, then cover it and read the characters.
6. **Finish every session with the Practice table.** Cover the right column and produce the answer from memory.

## Key Features

### Pattern Phrases (Templates)

This guide emphasizes **pattern phrases**: templates you can customize.

| Pattern | Chinese | Pinyin | Example |
|---------|---------|--------|---------|
| Where is [place]? | [地方]在哪儿？ | [dìfang] zài nǎr? | 地铁站在哪儿？ (Dìtiě zhàn zài nǎr?) = Where is the subway station? |
| Can I [action]? | 我可以[动作]吗？ | Wǒ kěyǐ [dòngzuò] ma? | 我可以坐这儿吗？ (Wǒ kěyǐ zuò zhèr ma?) = Can I sit here? |
| I want [thing] | 我要[东西] | Wǒ yào [dōngxi] | 我要这个 (Wǒ yào zhège) = I want this one |

### Politeness Levels

Each chapter marks the appropriate **formality** in the Notes column:

- **Casual:** 你 (nǐ), short sentences. Use with friends, peers and younger people.
- **Polite:** 请 (qǐng), 麻烦你 (máfan nǐ), 不好意思 (bù hǎoyìsi). Use with strangers, service staff and colleagues.
- **Very polite or formal:** 您 (nín), titles after surnames, formal vocabulary. Use with seniors, clients and elders.

## A Note on Pinyin and Tones

Pinyin is the standard romanization of Mandarin. Every syllable carries a tone, shown by a mark over a vowel: mā, má, mǎ, mà. A syllable with no mark has the neutral tone.

This guide writes pinyin **as it is spoken**. Two common words change tone depending on the next syllable, and the pinyin shows the change:

| Word | Dictionary tone | Spoken example | Pinyin in this guide |
|------|-----------------|----------------|----------------------|
| 不 (not) | bù | 不是 (is not) | bú shì |
| 一 (one) | yī | 一个 (one of something) | yí ge |
| 一 (one) | yī | 一杯 (one cup) | yì bēi |

The change for two third tones in a row (你好 is spoken ní hǎo) is not written, by convention. Chapter 3 explains all three rules.

## A Note on Regional Variation

Mandarin is spoken across mainland China, Taiwan, Singapore and Malaysia, and by communities worldwide. This guide teaches the **mainland standard**. Taiwan uses traditional characters and some different everyday words (for example 捷运 instead of 地铁 for "subway"). The Notes column marks those differences, and Chapter 10 has a full comparison table. Traditional character forms appear only in Chapter 4.

Accents differ by region. Speakers everywhere understand the standard forms taught here.
