# S-M02 q18

**Claim.** $I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi}$.

**Proof (direct, by squaring).** First, $I$ converges: $0 < e^{-x^2} \le e^{1 - 2|x|}$ for all $x$ (since $x^2 \ge 2|x| - 1$), and $\int e^{1 - 2|x|} dx$ is finite. Because the integral converges absolutely, the product of two copies is a double integral over the plane (Fubini):
$$I^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2} dy = \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\,dx\,dy .$$
Change to polar coordinates $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, $r \ge 0$, $0 \le \theta < 2\pi$. The Jacobian determinant is $r$, so $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$, and $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$:
$$I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\, r\,dr\,d\theta .$$
The inner integral, with $s = r^2$ and $ds = 2r\,dr$, is $\frac12 \int_0^\infty e^{-s} ds = \frac12$. The outer integral of the constant $\frac12$ over $[0, 2\pi)$ is $\pi$. So $I^2 = \pi$. Since the integrand is positive, $I > 0$, hence $I = \sqrt{\pi}$.
